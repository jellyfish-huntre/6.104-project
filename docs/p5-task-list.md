# P5 user study tasks

This is the task plan I used for the two studies. The actual timing and deviations are recorded after the facilitator transitions.

Roam is meant to help someone find a conversation they did not schedule in advance, then keep exploring if the person they want to meet is busy. I planned to use the same tasks with two participants, allowing about 40 minutes for tasks and 20 minutes for a debrief. The times below were guides.

The participant opens [Roam](https://roam.mit-sdg.dev/) in a fresh guest session with the Demo Fair code `3BC7D6B4`. The six booths and the representative-controlled call states are described in the [study setup](p5-study-setup.md). I will ask the participant to think aloud, let them try to recover from difficulties, and record any help I give.

| Task | Instruction to the participant | Why I included it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Arrive at the fair (3 min) | “You're invited to the Demo Fair. Go ahead and see which groups are here.” | Tests whether a newcomer understands code entry and guest access. Watching shows which entry route they try and whether they look for account signup. |
| 2. Choose where to start (6 min) | “You are interested in making local transit easier to use. Find two groups whose work might fit, then decide which one you would approach first and why.” | Tests how visitors compare two plausible groups and whether they use the map, directory, or both to choose a route. |
| 3. Check an unavailable conversation (5 min) | “Go to the Campus Garden Lab booth and see if you can speak with someone there right now.” | Tests whether a booth without a live call looks unavailable, broken, or simply quiet to a visitor. |
| 4. Join an open conversation (8 min) | “You have a question for Lantern Robotics, ask someone whether beginners can join Thursday build nights.” | Tests where visitors expect the conversation to start and how they handle the move from Roam to an external meeting. |
| 5. Wait without standing still (9 min) | “Lantern Robotics is now speaking with someone else, but you still want a short one-to-one conversation. Join the queue, then go explore another booth.” | Tests whether visitors trust that their request remains while they explore. Because the instruction names the queue, it cannot test whether they discover that action on their own. |
| 6. Return for the conversation (7 min) | “Your fair page has reloaded while you were waiting. Find out whether you still have a turn and, if Lantern Robotics is ready for you, speak with them.” | Tests whether visitors can confirm their request survived reload, then recognize an invitation and open the private link. |
| 7. Finish the visit (2 min) | “You are done for now. Leave the fair.” | Tests whether leaving the fair is distinct from closing a call or booth panel and what visitors think happens to their presence. |

## Planned facilitator transitions

Before each session, I will verify the [Demo Fair setup](p5-study-setup.md): six booths are visible, Lantern Robotics has an open group call, and the participant has no earlier request. I will be present as the Lantern Robotics representative in the study-controlled meeting, so task 4 can involve a real conversation. After task 4, I will switch that booth to invite-only mode with the separate private meeting link. This is a planned state change, not an action the participant must discover. Task 5 uses the scripted instruction above.

After the participant requests a turn and has spent about one minute exploring another booth, I will send an invitation from the representative session. At the start of task 6, I will reload the participant's fair page to create the stated situation. I will use the same timing and sequence in both studies, and note any deviation. If a participant cannot complete a task, I will record where they stopped and any intervention before moving on; the next task should not be reported as independent success if I had to set it up for them.

The final 20 minutes are for questions about moments I observed: what the participant expected, what they tried, and how they interpreted Roam's response. I will keep observations separate from my explanations when writing the two study reports.

## What happened in the sessions

Both participants consented to the study. Each session took about 30 minutes for the tasks and debrief together, rather than the planned hour. I gave the prompts and help described in the [study reports](p5-study-reports.md); in particular, I explained Roam's general premise to Participant 1 during exploration but did not point out controls. I did tell both participants to “join the queue” in task 5. That means their success on that task does not show they could find the queue without being told what to do. Participant 2's earlier, unprompted queue click is the better evidence for that question.

I also changed the invitation sequence from the plan above. I let each participant reload and check whether their queue request was still there, then sent the invitation and had them open the private call. The invitation was therefore still active when they needed it. This sequence tested persistence after reload and recovery of the private link, but it did not test whether someone would notice an invitation that arrived while they were away from the booth.
