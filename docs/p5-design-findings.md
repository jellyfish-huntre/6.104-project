# P5 design findings

Both students completed the visitor tasks, but they took different routes through the fair. One tried to interact with tables before he understood what booths were for. The other learned about groups by walking into booths and reading the descriptions that appeared. I ordered these findings by what I would work on or test first. Neither session supports a major or critical rating.

## 1. A booth's purpose was unclear in an empty fair

**Level:** Conceptual: he misunderstood what a booth was for. **Severity:** Moderate: I explained Roam's purpose before the remaining tasks, so I did not see him recover on his own. He could still explore, so I cannot call it a complete block.

Participant 1 clicked tables and walked into them, then asked, “what do I do at booths?” He later said he thought Roam was a game where he interacted with the booth itself. Once I explained the fair's purpose, he completed the remaining tasks without help. The moment matters because Roam depends on visitors understanding that booths connect them with people.

The quiet study setup may have caused some of this: there were no other visitors at the booths. Still, a real fair could be quiet too. I would try a short first-visit explanation that connects booths to people and conversations, then test it in another quiet fair without verbally explaining the premise. I would compare that with clearer booth text or a visible representative before deciding which cue does the most work.

## 2. The directory was easy to miss, although walking worked

**Level:** Physical: the directory is a symbol button at the lower left of the map. **Severity:** Minor: walking let both participants find booths without getting stuck.

Neither participant opened the directory during exploration. Participant 2 noticed it afterward and said the icon was not very visible or obvious to click. She suggested moving it to the upper left and making it stand out more. But walking may have been enough for her: booth descriptions appeared as she entered, and she used them to decide what to explore. Participant 1 never commented on the directory, so I cannot say he found it hard to locate.

I would give the control a visible “Booth directory” label and try a more prominent location. I would test this with a task that asks a new visitor to find a particular booth, then watch whether they use the directory, walk there, or switch between the two. That would show whether the problem is visibility or simply that walking is the better route for this kind of visit.

## 3. The queue is understandable, but its language and progress could be clearer

**Level:** Linguistic for “Request a turn”; conceptual for knowing one's place while waiting. **Severity:** Minor: Participant 2 understood the button, and neither participant reported trouble waiting.

During the first exploration task, Participant 2 clicked “Request a turn” before she was asked to join a queue and understood what had happened. I later told both participants to join the queue, so only her early click tells me anything about discovering the button. “Join the queue” would describe the action more directly. Once someone joins, the view says “You are waiting” and encourages exploration, but does not show a place in line. Nobody complained about that in these sessions; we did not test a busy queue.

I would try “Join the queue” and show a position such as “1st in queue” while the request is active. In a follow-up with several real people waiting, I would ask visitors what they think will happen next and whether they feel comfortable leaving the booth. This matters more in a busy fair than in our study's short, controlled wait.

## 4. Traveling across a larger fair may become slow

**Level:** Physical: this concerns how far a visitor must move through the map. **Severity:** Provisionally minor: neither participant had trouble traveling in this fair.

When I asked for improvements afterward, Participant 2 suggested sprinting or jumping to a booth from the directory because crossing a larger map might feel slow. She did not have trouble getting around the six-booth Demo Fair. Walking was also how she found descriptions, so a shortcut could remove some useful discovery.

I would first test a longer route in a larger fair and watch whether visitors get impatient, miss booths, or use the directory. If travel becomes a real obstacle, I would try a “go to booth” action from the directory and check whether visitors can still explore naturally. I would not change movement speed based only on this prediction.
