# P5 design findings

The clearest moment in the studies was Participant 1 walking into a table because he thought the booth itself was something to interact with. These findings come from two students who might attend a club or career fair. Both completed the visitor tasks. I ordered the list by what I would work on or test first, not by how many features they suggested. None of the observations supports a major or critical rating.

## 1. A booth's purpose was unclear in an empty fair

**Level:** Conceptual, because the visitor misunderstood what a booth is for. **Severity:** Moderate, because that misunderstanding affected Roam's main purpose and I explained the premise before he continued. He was still able to explore, so I cannot say it completely blocked him.

During exploration, Participant 1 clicked tables and walked into them. He asked, “what do I do at booths?” and later said he thought Roam was a game where he interacted with the booth itself. I explained that Roam is an online club or career fair before the rest of the tasks. He then completed them without help. His initial understanding matters because finding people to talk to is Roam's main purpose, and the study does not show that he would have figured it out alone.

The quiet study setup may have caused some of this: there were no other visitors at the booths. Still, a real fair could be quiet too. I would try a short first-visit explanation that connects booths to people and conversations, then test it in another quiet fair without verbally explaining the premise. I would compare that with clearer booth text or a visible representative before deciding which cue does the most work.

## 2. The directory was easy to miss, although walking worked

**Level:** Physical, because the directory's icon and position made the control harder to notice. **Severity:** Minor, because both participants could still find booths by walking.

Neither participant opened the booth directory during exploration. Participant 2 noticed it afterward and said its icon was not very visible or obvious to click. She suggested moving it to the upper left and making it stand out more. This is evidence that the entry point could be clearer, but it was not a task failure: both participants preferred walking, and Participant 2 learned about booths from descriptions that popped up as she entered them. Participant 1 never commented on the directory, so I cannot say he found it hard to locate.

I would give the control a visible “Booth directory” label and try a more prominent location. I would test this with a task that asks a new visitor to find a particular booth, then watch whether they use the directory, walk there, or switch between the two. That would show whether the problem is visibility or simply that walking is the better route for this kind of visit.

## 3. The queue is understandable, but its language and progress could be clearer

**Level:** Linguistic for the button's wording; conceptual for understanding progress while waiting. **Severity:** Minor, because the early queue click showed understanding and neither participant reported trouble waiting. The missing position is still untested with a real line of visitors.

During the first exploration task, Participant 2 clicked “Request a turn” before she was asked to join a queue and understood what had happened. I later told both participants to join the queue, so those later successes cannot establish whether the button is discoverable on its own. The early click argues against calling the wording a serious flaw. Still, “Join the queue” would name the action more directly. The waiting view says “You are waiting” and encourages exploration, but does not show the visitor's place in line. Neither participant reported being confused by that missing information, so this is an opportunity to test, not a demonstrated problem.

I would try “Join the queue” and show a position such as “1st in queue” while the request is active. In a follow-up with several real people waiting, I would ask visitors what they think will happen next and whether they feel comfortable leaving the booth. This matters more in a busy fair than in our study's short, controlled wait.

## 4. Traveling across a larger fair may become slow

**Level:** Physical, because this concerns the distance a visitor must move through the map. **Severity:** Minor as a tentative rating: no travel difficulty occurred in these sessions.

When I asked for improvements afterward, Participant 2 suggested sprinting or jumping to a booth from the directory because crossing a larger map might feel slow. She did not have trouble getting around the six-booth Demo Fair. Walking was also how she found descriptions, so a shortcut could remove some useful discovery.

I would first test a longer route in a larger fair and watch whether visitors get impatient, miss booths, or use the directory. If travel becomes a real obstacle, I would try a “go to booth” action from the directory and check whether visitors can still explore naturally. I would not change movement speed based only on this prediction.
